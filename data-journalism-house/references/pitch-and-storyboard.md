# Pitch, kill checkpoints, archetypes, and storyboard

## The pitch form (complete in writing before drafting)

After The Pudding's pitch gate. A blank or weak answer to Q2, Q5, or Q6 kills or defers
the piece.

1. **ELEVATOR PITCH** — What is the story about? (≤3 sentences)
2. **SINGLE POINT** — The one point you're making, in ONE sentence. This becomes the
   working headline.
3. **WHY YOU / WHY THIS OUTLET** — What access, data, or obsession makes this yours?
   (An outlet with members it can phone, or data nobody else holds, should use that
   advantage — a piece any think tank could produce squanders it.)
4. **DIFFERENTIATION** — What assumption does the thesis challenge? A good story has a
   thesis that invites debate.
5. **AHA MOMENT** — The specific surprising element the audience will remember. If the
   analysis isn't done yet: what result WOULD constitute it, and what result kills the
   piece? (Lookup/tracker archetypes answer with UTILITY instead: the question the
   reader will return to, and the verdict sentence the piece computes for them.)
6. **DATA ROADMAP** — Named, feasible source(s) and the path from raw data to the claim
   (fields, joins, the key computed measure). Does the dataset exist or must it be built?
   Is it *ethical* to collect and use — not merely legal?
7. **CORE VISUAL** — Sketch the centerpiece: which of the 9 relationships it shows, the
   chart form, and what the annotated takeaway will say if the hypothesis holds.
8. **MEDIUM CHECK** — scoped by archetype (SKILL.md stage 1): for a VISUAL ESSAY, The
   Pudding's bar applies — "if your idea would make an incredible podcast, video, or
   standard 1,000-word text reporting," it fails. For the default explainer the bar is:
   at least one chart does argumentative work prose cannot.
9. **CLAIM TYPE** — descriptive / comparative / explanatory / causal / predictive /
   normative. This caps the verbs you may use later (see writing.md ladder) and prevents
   descriptive data quietly becoming a causal conclusion.
10. **FALSIFIER** — What evidence would make you abandon or substantially revise this
    story? Writing it down converts advocacy-with-charts into an investigation.
11. **ANCHOR** — One concrete thing a reader can hold: a place, a year, an institution, a
    scene. Not a rate. "The flagship graduated two polymer undergraduates in 2023" carries a
    piece; "completions fell 61%" does not. **If there is none, record that as a REPORTING
    GAP here rather than inventing one** — the absence is usually a gap in the reporting, not
    in the writing, and naming it is what sends someone to find it.

    Added 2026-08-30. It was the one field of a rival five-line commission form that this
    ten-field pitch did not already cover, and it survived a cross-model review that killed
    the other four as redundant against Q2, Q7 and Q10.

11. **WHY NOW** — the peg (event, data release, season, anniversary) or an explicit
    evergreen declaration; if evergreen, what future moment would make it land harder,
    and is holding worth it? Peg-vs-evergreen tradecraft: a pegged piece ships smaller
    and faster; a finished evergreen can wait for its peg; the tracker archetype exists
    because some pegs recur.
12. **DECLARE THE PUBLICATION MODE, then name the implied reader.** Two modes, and they
    bind different rules. Getting this wrong in either direction wastes work: a house
    organ built to newsroom standards pays for reporting it will never do, and a
    newsroom piece built to house-organ standards gets read as PR.

    - **JOURNALISM MODE** — the piece addresses a public with no stake in the publisher.
      Named-source reporting is expected (writing.md's rung-1 ladder and its portfolio
      limit apply), the publisher is a subject like any other, and an outside antagonist
      is legitimate.
    - **INSTITUTIONAL MODE** — an organization publishing rigorous evidence about its own
      domain: a capability demonstration, a decision aid, an evidence base for claims the
      organization makes. Named-source reporting is NOT required and composites are
      acceptable when labeled. What replaces reporting as the credibility mechanism is
      transparency plus falsifiability: the reader can check the work.

    In BOTH modes, one rule holds — **never publish the instrumentation.** A sentence
    that hands the reader a talking point ("the sentence we can carry into a funder
    meeting", "the argument to lead with in that conversation") shows outsiders the
    machinery pointed at them, and the people most likely to read it are the people it
    names. This is a persuasion failure before it is an ethics one. The distinction that
    matters is direction: analysis that **corrects the publisher's own claim** ("we
    should retire the word 'cheap' and argue the checkable version") is an asset in both
    modes, because it proves the evidence is not being managed. Keep the corrections;
    cut the coaching.

## Continue / pivot / put-it-down checkpoints

Run in order; stop or reframe at the first failure (The Pudding's process):

1. Is the question unique — or can you at least tell it a new way?
2. Do the data exist, or can you feasibly build the dataset?
3. Is it ethical to use? Legal availability is not ethical justification (scraped
   personal data, inferred demographics, vulnerable groups).
4. After analysis: **was the answer surprising, or useful?** (Lookup/tracker archetypes
   pass on utility.) If the data merely confirm the obvious, kill — or reframe around
   the most surprising sub-finding WITH the HARKing guard from SKILL.md stage 1: a
   scanned-for surprise is selected noise until it persists; label the reframe
   exploratory and test persistence before headlining it.
5. Is this your story to tell, or does it need other voices?
6. After the storyboard: re-answer pitch Q2 (single point) and Q5 (aha) from the
   storyboard alone. If the aha sentence changed, weakened, or now needs qualifying,
   pivot or kill.
7. After the build: same probe against the built page. If a reader of the draft could
   not state the single point, the build failed the pitch — fix or pivot.

(These seven checkpoints re-apply SKILL.md stage 1's kill criteria at later moments in
the pipeline — same criteria, later evidence; if the two texts ever seem to disagree,
stage 1 governs.)

Saying goodbye, pivoting, and continuing are all acceptable at every juncture. A killed
story after honest analysis is evidence the process works; a rescued pitch that tortures
the data is the genre's cardinal sin.

## Archetype library

Pick ONE primary archetype at storyboard time. Hybrids exist but a piece that doesn't know
its archetype turns into an inventory.

### 1. Data-led explainer (THE DEFAULT) — seven-beat skeleton

Modeled on Upshot craft. Each beat = one section; most beats = one chart.

- **BEAT 1 — HOOK** (1–2 paras, no chart or one small chart): the sledgehammer stat or a
  single concrete case embodying the finding. State the piece's single point plainly.
  Never open with methodology, background history, or "we analyzed X million rows" —
  UNLESS the collection feat is itself the surprise (a corpus nobody has built, a
  measurement nobody thought to take), in which case the feat opens, stated as a feat
  ("we measured 80 pairs of jeans"), never as apparatus ("our methodology involved…").
- **BEAT 2 — THE FINDING** (core chart): the takeaway-titled centerpiece; prose states the
  finding, directs the eye, names the size of the effect.
- **BEAT 3 — HOW WE KNOW** (short, in-text): the provenance sentence — who compiled the
  data, what the measure counts, who analyzed and who reviewed. Pre-empts "says who?"
  One or two sentences here; the full apparatus lives in the methodology box.
- **BEAT 4 — COMPARED WITH WHAT** (chart): history, peers, or distribution (percentiles,
  not just the average). Show where the finding is strongest and weakest; surface the
  sub-group where the story really lives.
- **BEAT 5 — WHY** (claim-type-gated; see writing.md's WHY discipline): for
  explanatory/causal pieces, the signposted mechanism section — "*N* broad factors
  appear to be driving [finding]," one sub-section per cause with its own evidence,
  verbs per the ladder. For descriptive/comparative pieces this beat shrinks to one
  honest accounting sentence (known / contested-and-attributed / unknown) inside an
  adjacent beat — never a manufactured mechanism section. Two to four factors; more than
  four, group them. In the storyboard Beat 5 may split into factor cards, grouped to at
  most 3 so the storyboard stays within its 5–9 card window.
- **BEAT 6 — CAVEATS & COUNTER-CASES**: where the data end, what the finding does NOT
  say, the strongest good-faith objection and your answer, uncertainty with its
  complement. This is EDITORIAL narrative — the objection-and-answer is story, written
  in body register; the per-figure caveat budgets in page-design.md govern apparatus ink
  (source lines, limitation notes), not this beat.
- **BEAT 7 — SO WHAT / KICKER** (1–2 paras): implications, trajectory, or a scope-limiting
  reframe. End on a concrete image or number, not a platitude. The kicker RESOLVES the
  hero's question.
- **+ METHODOLOGY BOX** between the final beat and the closer element (canonical page
  order, all files: story beats → methodology → closer → footer; the house stack's
  `.pv-method` injecting before `.closer` implements exactly this).

Ordering law: finding before cause; evidence before interpretation; caveats before
implications; never open with Beat 3.

### 2. Participatory / prior-elicitation piece

Use when the finding contradicts a common intuition AND the medium supports commitment.
(Modeled on Upshot "You Draw It".)

- **SETUP**: pose the question; give only the axes/context needed to guess. Never
  foreshadow the answer — the headline poses, never answers.
- **ELICIT**: the reader commits — draws a line, moves a slider, picks an option. In
  non-interactive media: "Before reading on, write down your guess: ___."
- **REVEAL** in three steps: (a) truth overlaid on their guess; (b) the canonical wrong
  belief — "Most readers guessed X; the reality is Y"; (c) one sentence naming the
  character of the surprise.
- **WHY THE INTUITION FAILS**: mechanism behind the true pattern, and why the prior exists.
- **STRESS TEST**: does it hold across sub-groups/time? Where does it break?
- **SO WHAT**: the corrected belief, stated.
- **+ METHODOLOGY BOX.**

The elicitation IS the "something spectacular" that justifies interaction (Tse). If it is
removed, rebuild on the seven-beat skeleton — don't just strip it.

### 3. Personal lookup / "story about you"

Population data → an individual result: your county, your occupation, your metro, your
team. The page tells the national story with a strong default (nothing behind the lookup
that the story needs), and the lookup re-tells it from the reader's seat: highlighted rows
across every chart, one templated verdict sentence recomputed ("A $1,332 Akron paycheck
buys what $1,690 buys in Chicago"). For a membership or regional audience this is the
highest-leverage archetype upgrade: it converts an essay into a tool people reopen, and
its natural companion is a shareable artifact (a per-unit "card" with the verdict sentence
and source line) that lets the piece travel into the conversation it was built for.

### 4. Maintained data product / tracker

A model, average, ranking, or tracker that updates. Passes the pitch gate on UTILITY
(a question readers return to), not on an aha. Don't build this archetype casually — it
is a maintenance commitment, not an article.

**Operational contract** (all required before first publish):
- Beat skeleton: topline + what changed since last update → how it works, in reader
  language → track record (a named accuracy/calibration statistic and, where
  probabilistic, a calibration or backtest plot) → what would change the reading → 
  methodology.
- Frozen target definition (what exactly is being predicted/tracked, and when it
  resolves); documented input pipeline (inclusion rules, weights, recency handling).
- Versioning: model/method changes dated and explained, never silent; an update log
  distinct from the corrections log (writing.md).
- Update discipline: cadence stated on the page ("data as of…"), rules for late/revised
  inputs, and — for probabilistic products — a standing Layer-2 review item: calibration
  reported against outcomes as they resolve.
- Methodology box adds three fields: model version + date; inputs/training window; and
  the backtest or calibration summary.

### 5. Visual essay

The Pudding's form: a narrow thesis decomposed into visual beats, prose as connective
tissue between demonstrations. Justified when the visual experience IS the argument
(a form so fitted to the material that a text version loses the point). Highest
craft cost; storyboard discipline is mandatory, and the pitch's medium check must have a
strong answer.

### 6. Methodology story

The evidence pipeline itself is the news ("how we measured X; what the standard method
hides"). Legitimate and distinctive — but it must still open with what the reader learns,
not how the sausage is made, and it needs a "what to point this instrument at next" close.
Do not let every piece drift into this archetype: method-as-protagonist is a failure mode
everywhere except here.

## Storyboard beat card

One card per beat, written AFTER analysis and BEFORE prose. A complete storyboard is 5–9
cards a colleague could read as a coherent argument with no charts drawn.

Two practical notes: (1) If you must storyboard before the analysis exists (a pitch-stage
storyboard), mark every number and annotation as [PLACEHOLDER], put what-the-analysis-
could-do-to-this-card in RISK, and RE-VALIDATE every card once real numbers arrive —
a placeholder storyboard is a plan, not a storyboard. (2) If a house stack applies, read
its stack reference BEFORE choosing chart forms — a form the stack cannot cheaply render
(a map, a scrolly, small multiples) is a storyboard-time discovery, not a build-time one.

```
BEAT #__ of __
- CLAIM (one sentence — becomes the section's chart title or opening sentence):
- EVIDENCE (dataset + specific fields/slice that carry the claim):
- RELATIONSHIP (one of 9: change / magnitude / ranking / distribution / correlation /
  deviation / part-to-whole / spatial / flow):
- CHART FORM (from the chart-craft decision table; "none — prose only" is allowed):
- ANNOTATION TEXT (the literal in-plot annotation naming what the reader should see;
  "none — the title carries it" is legal for ≤3-mark forms per chart-craft):
- COPY SUMMARY (2–3 sentences: state claim → direct the eye → bridge):
- TRANSITION (why the NEXT beat follows — what question this beat leaves open):
- RISK (what in the data could invalidate this beat; what you'll check before building):
```

Assembly checks:

- Every card advances the pitch's single point.
- The organizing question is planted in the hero card and RESOLVED by the closer card.
- The aha card appears by card 2–3; at least one later card escalates or complicates it.
- No two adjacent cards repeat the same CLAIM with near-identical charts — merge or cut
  ("one good chart is better than seven okay charts" — Amanda Cox). Exception:
  deliberate repetition-with-escalation (page-design.md), where the repeated FORM is the
  argument and a single variable steps card to card.
- One human-scale card; one caveat card before the final so-what card.
- Each section makes exactly one claim, carried by at most one chart (chartless beats —
  hook, provenance, kicker — are normal); prose does three jobs — state the claim,
  direct the eye, bridge to the next claim.
