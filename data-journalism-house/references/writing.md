# Writing: headlines, standfirsts, voice, uncertainty, methodology

(Worked examples in this file draw on a regional-economics evidence site; the patterns
generalize to culture, science, and sports pieces — substitute the domain, keep the move.)

## The basis lint (run it over every claim sentence)

The single most repeated defect class a 56-review cross-model audit found — six pages of
thirteen — was a figure whose stated BASIS or UNIT did not match the sentence built on it:
a median described as an average, jobs counted while people were named, "half of it" where
the antecedent basis made it 29 percent, and the same named quantity printed as 1.21× and
1.24× on two pages from two undisclosed populations. The lint: every ratio, share, or
median sentence names its population and basis IN the sentence (median of what, share of
what, against which baseline), and any quantity printed on two surfaces either shares a
basis or names both. Where a stack has a cross-page figure registry, register it; where it
doesn't, the two surfaces WILL drift.

## Headline formulas (takeaway headlines)

A data-journalism headline states the finding, poses the reader's question, or offers
participation — never the topic. Draft at least 10 candidates across at least three
formulas before choosing:

1. **REVERSAL / LOST DISTINCTION**: "[Subject], long [old status], is no longer [status]"
   → *The American Middle Class Is No Longer the World's Richest* (Upshot). Variants:
   "X Is No Longer Y", "The End of X", "X Has Quietly Become Y".
2. **QUANTIFIED CLAIM**: the finding stated with a number or comparative verb →
   *Women's Pockets Are Inferior* (The Pudding); *The region recorded 111,500 hires to
   end up with 168 more jobs*. Rule: use a verb; superlatives only if literally true in
   the data AND the extreme survived the winner's-curse check (SKILL.md stage 3) — the
   max of many scanned cells is technically "literally true" and still misleading.
3. **PARTICIPATORY IMPERATIVE**: "You Draw It: [question the reader will answer]" — the
   headline poses, never answers, the question. (This formula, not the takeaway formulas,
   governs reveal-structured pieces; see the deepest rule's either/or in SKILL.md.)
4. **QUESTION-AS-PEG** (only when the answer is genuinely contested or surprising):
   "Why Do [surprising pattern]?" / "Who Actually [does X]?". The archetype picks the
   formula family: explainers default to 1–2; participatory pieces use 3; lookups use 5.
   When an archetype pulls toward a question but the data supports a clean quantified
   claim, the claim wins — questions are for pieces whose payoff is the reveal.
5. **PERSONAL LOOKUP**: "How [Your County / Your Age / Your Team] [verb-phrase]" —
   signals the reader will find themselves in the data.

**Two headline slots, not one**: the DISPLAY headline (voice, the formulas above) and a
findable SEO/meta title (plain nouns a searcher would type). Write both; they are
different jobs. Name the intended share image (the hero or centerpiece chart) and check
it reads standalone at social-card size.

**Bans**: topic-label headlines ("Income Inequality Trends"), "A Look At…",
"Exploring…", "…: A Data Analysis", analyst jargon as the headline's core noun
("measurable cells"), and any headline that would survive unchanged if the finding were
reversed — that is the test of whether it is a title or a label.

Headline honesty: never headline the fact the body then proves is unremarkable; headline
the actual finding. And never promise scope the page doesn't deliver ("Every state…" over
a top-20 chart).

## Standfirst / dek formula

One to two sentences under the headline, three jobs in order:

**[Sharpest supporting fact beyond the headline, with a number] + [source/scope
credential] + [stakes or reversal-of-expectation].**

Fill-in: "[Concrete instance of the finding, with number]. A new analysis of [scope:
years, units, records] shows [the general pattern] — and [the twist: who it contradicts,
what it reverses, or why now]."

Rules:
- At least one number; no methodology detail beyond the source's name. (A single
  apparatus stat in the hero stat row as a trust signature — "69 claims, each
  re-runnable" — is allowed; the Beat-1 ban is on opening PROSE with apparatus.)
- **Stakes before self-audit** — the reader's live question before the outlet's
  institutional framing. Keep the self-audit if it earns trust; it follows the stake.
- Maximum ONE caveat clause above the fold; the full caveat set lives beside the charts
  it qualifies and in the methodology box.
- Never pre-narrate every section. For takeaway pieces: state the finding. For
  reveal-structured pieces: pose the question, promise the twist, stop.
- One bold phrase maximum.
- The standfirst must not restate the headline in longer words.

## The audience contract (explanation depth)

The default reader is a **smart non-expert**. Every piece must answer, somewhere the
reader will actually meet it, all five reader questions:

1. **What is this?** — every measure, dataset, and term of art translated at first
   contact, in plain words. If the piece's core noun is analyst jargon, rename it in
   reader language and demote the term to a stat card. Constructed units get their own
   treatment below; they are the genre's most common comprehension defect.
2. **Why should the reader care?** — stakes in the standfirst, in the reader's own
   terms.
3. **Why is it happening?** — accounted for, at the depth the claim type supports (see
   the WHY discipline below). "Nobody knows yet" is an acceptable, sometimes excellent,
   answer; an unexplained inventory with no acknowledgment of the question is not.
4. **Compared with what?** — the benchmark that tells the reader whether the number is
   remarkable.
5. **How sure are we?** — calibrated, near the claim it qualifies.

Length discipline: spend words on stakes, mechanism, translation, and human scale; SAVE
words on apparatus, which moves to collapsed layers. If a draft feels long, cut apparatus
and hedge-repetition — not the explaining. Exemplar pages from 8 to 69 viewports all read
as flowing; density cadence and explanation completeness, not word count, are the
constraints.

## Constructed units: the reading, not the recipe

A **constructed unit** is any number that is not a plain count or a plain dollar amount:
ratios (1.2×, 0.88×), location quotients, indices (Jan 2019 = 100), rebased series,
quotients of quotients, shares, rates per thousand, ranks, percentiles, z-scores,
"points" of difference, retracement percentages. These are where a page becomes
unreadable while every number in it stays correct, and it is the most common
comprehension defect in the genre: in one audited site, 78 of 129 constructed units
failed the test below.

**The test**: a reader who has never met this measure can say what higher and lower mean,
and what the subject's own value amounts to in plain terms.

The rules, each earned from a real failure:

- **Direction of MAGNITUDE is not direction of MERIT, and a reader needs both.** "Higher
  means more concentrated here than in the country" tells a reader which way the number
  goes. It does not tell them whether to be pleased. Naive readers stall on exactly this:
  they can read the axis and still cannot say whether the page is delivering good news.
  Say plainly what a high value means for the subject, including when the honest answer
  is that it cuts both ways ("concentration is what makes the region distinctive, and it
  is also what makes it exposed to one industry's downturn"). A measure whose merit
  direction is genuinely contested is worth a sentence, never silence.
- **A derived number must follow from the numbers printed beside it.** If an annotation
  reads "13.5% of 402 jobs" and prints 434, a reader does the arithmetic, gets 54, and
  stops trusting the page. Any hidden multiplier — annualising, both sides of a flow,
  four quarters, a rate applied to a different base — is stated where the number is, or
  the number is restated on a base the reader can see.
- **The measure's name is not its meaning.** "Location quotient, region against the
  nation" and "Index, January 2019 = 100" say what the number is called or how it was
  built. Neither says which way is up. When a scale is constructed, the axis title states
  the reading ("more concentrated here than in the country →"); the measure's name and
  its formula move to the source line or methodology box.
- **Label a reference line by what crossing it means**, not by what it equals. "1.0× is
  the national share" is true and still hides the direction; "1.0× · above it, more
  concentrated here" reads.
- **Plain leads at FIRST use, and first use is rarely the chart.** It is usually a hero
  stat card or a headline, where there is no axis to hang direction on and the author
  feels least room. Find the earliest occurrence in READING order, not the place the
  author first needed the term, and spend that slot's whole sub-line on the plain
  reading. Being plain somewhere later does not count. The technical form then survives
  and every later reference can use it bare.
- **Chart chrome is prose.** Axis titles, table headers, tooltips, legend text, and
  stat-card labels get the same first-contact translation as body text. They are exactly
  where a reader lands without the paragraph that would have explained them.
- **A rank is a constructed unit.** It carries which end is good ("1 = highest pay") and
  its universe ("of 155 metros that disclose"), or it is not a rank.
- **Name the denominator when it changes.** On a page where every "×" means "times the
  national share", a "2.1×" that means "times the next-highest industry" is a trap.
  Say "2.1 times the second industry" in words, every time.
- **One glyph, one unit per page.** Once "×" is spent on one constructed measure, a
  second unrelated ratio converts to percent or plain words.
- **Size a difference unit before spending it.** "Points" needs one clause saying what a
  point is ("one job in a hundred") at first use.
- **Transformed scales need a reading aid, not just disclosure.** "(square-root scale)"
  in an axis title occupies the space the direction needed; put the transform in the
  source line and give the reader one applicable sentence ("twice as far right is four
  times as many").
- **An out-of-range value earns its own clause.** A share above 100%, an index below its
  base, a negative count: say what being past the bound means rather than letting a
  correct oddity stand.
- **A truncated axis on a constructed unit deletes the reader's anchor.** If the
  reference value (1.0×, the base year, zero) falls outside the drawn domain, say so on
  the figure.
- **Expand your own name first.** The acronym an organisation uses hourly is the one it
  never sees, and it will appear a dozen times unexpanded, including inside data labels
  ("PIC-12"), on the page arguing for that organisation's credibility. Expand the
  publisher's name at first contact like any other term, and expand the acronyms that
  carry the piece's subject (the awarding agency, the programme, the survey) even when
  the whole team says them aloud daily.
- **One noun, one referent, per piece.** A page used "events" for dated public
  occurrences and for job starts-and-ends in consecutive sentences, and used "fourteen
  occupations" for two different sets under adjacent headings. Where two things need
  the same word, rename one and say which is which the first time both appear. This is
  the noun version of the one-glyph rule.
- **Deliver what the label promises.** A kicker reading "Where Northeast Ohio actually
  sits" over a page whose every figure is a state or a metro; navigation reading
  "Interactive evidence" over a page with no visualisation at all. Check the promise in
  the navigation, the kicker, the headline and the section head against what the page
  actually contains, and change whichever is wrong — usually the promise.
- **A re-layout that changes a chart changes its prose.** When the mobile variant rotates
  a chart or swaps its form, any sentence describing the desktop orientation is now false
  on a phone. Sentences that describe a form live with that form, not with the page.
- **Terms of art arrive looking like English.** *Cell, pairing, base, establishment,
  print, disclosed, withheld, seasonally adjusted, real, nominal, window, retracement.*
  Each is one clause from readable. Sweep for them at first contact, and when a piece
  defines a word technically, grep the page for that word in its ordinary sense and
  rename the ordinary uses.
- **Pay for reading by cutting apparatus first, and if it still costs length, spend it.**
  Look at what the same figure is saying twice before adding words; duplicated caveats
  usually fund the whole translation. But clarity outranks the length budget: a page is
  never improved by leaving a number unreadable to hold a word count. The ink budgets in
  page-design.md govern apparatus, never the reading.
- **A label that grows must be re-rendered at 375px.** A translation that fixes desktop
  and overlaps on mobile has moved the defect, not fixed it.

## The WHY discipline (mechanism without manufactured causation)

Gate the mechanism beat on claim type (pitch Q9):

- **Explanatory/causal pieces**: Beat 5 is required — "N broad factors appear to be
  driving [finding]," one sub-section per factor with its own evidence, hedged verbs per
  the ladder.
- **Descriptive/comparative pieces**: one honest accounting sentence suffices — the why
  is known-and-obvious (say it in a clause), contested (name the candidate explanations
  and attribute them to whoever holds them), or unknown (say so; that is itself a
  finding). Never invent a mechanism section to make a descriptive piece feel like
  journalism — forcing explanation without evidence is the genre sin, not skipping WHY.
- The hedge "appears to drive" is reserved for the enumerated-causes beat AND requires
  mechanism evidence beyond the correlation itself (expert judgment, prior literature, a
  temporal or dose pattern) — cited in the same section. With correlation alone, use
  association verbs and say the causal question is open.

## Voice

The register: conversational but numerate; confident about the evidence actually
obtained; explicit about assumptions; resistant to false balance and equally resistant
to false certainty. Authoritative without sounding omniscient.

- Plain, active sentences. "Sales increased 32%, from $1.8M to $2.4M" — never "a 32%
  increase in sales was observed."
- Translate jargon at first contact, in-line and in plain words ("LODES — a Census file
  that records, for every job, where the worker lives").
- Round numbers to argument precision (up to 3 significant figures unless the argument
  turns on the next digit): 43%, 12.8k, $1.2m. Translate to human units: weekly wages
  annualized; an index stat gets its plain reading first ("gas now costs less than
  before the spike") with the technical figure as the sub-line.
- **Percent vs percentage points, always**: a rate moving 4% → 6% rose two percentage
  points (or 50 percent); never write "rose 2%" for a 2-point move.
- Kill the failure registers found in real drafts: **seminar-creep** (chart-construction
  rationale in section titles), **prosecutorial tics** ("we are not going to tell you"),
  **hedge repetition** (each qualifier once per reading context), **compression
  overload** (40-word em-dash chains).
- Calibration prior, not a quota: agent first drafts over-connect — expect connective
  and editorializing tissue to roughly halve under a real edit; stakes, mechanism,
  translation, and human scale are exempt from the cut.

## The prose engine (how genre paragraphs actually move)

The signature pattern, at paragraph and section scale:

**Claim → evidence → interpretation → limitation → next question.**

Not: background → more background → methodological throat-clearing → chart → conclusion.
Strong takeaway pieces reveal the central finding early, then earn and qualify it. Each
paragraph does one move; the limitation rides WITH the claim it limits, and the "next
question" clause pulls the reader into the following section — sections end on momentum,
not on a caveat.

Sentence music, the part templates can't do alone:

- **Vary the gait.** After a long evidence sentence, land a short declarative. A page of
  uniform 35-word sentences reads as a memo; a page of fragments reads as a deck.
- **Give numbers verbs.** Prices *round-trip*, ranks *climb*, gaps *stick* — wherever
  the data support the motion.
- **One aphorism per act, maximum.** The quotable line is the genre's signature and it
  devalues fast; spend it in the closer or at the aha — and build it as a direct claim,
  never as a contrastive inversion (style-bans.md outlaws "not X, but Y" structures).
- **Ledes may withhold, headlines may not tease falsely.** A section lede can pose the
  question its chart answers — but the answer arrives within that section.
- **First-person policy is a decision, not a default.** "We" is the honest voice for
  judgment calls ("we chose medians because…") and forbidden as a hedge ("we feel
  that…"). Decide once on the voice card.

## The voice card (outlet identity — write once, apply to every piece)

The genre register is shared; each OUTLET holds its own identity constant. Before a
first piece, write a short voice card and reuse it: REGISTER (newsroom or essay);
PRONOUNS ("we"? direct address?); HUMOR/PLAY (none / dry / full personality); SIGNATURE
MOVES (the 2–3 recurring devices that make the outlet recognizable — protect these
during edits; they are identity, not clutter); BANNED IN THIS HOUSE (the outlet's tic
list, appended as discovered); CREDITS (byline format: "By X · Analysis Y · Graphics Z"
— multi-role credit is genre furniture).

For pieces in John's personal or business voice (not an outlet's editorial voice),
compose with the `swanson-writing` skill — the rules here still govern structure,
claims, and uncertainty; that skill governs diction.

## Attribution grammar (where fact ends and inference begins)

- "**The records show…**" — direct observation in the data (and note: this asserts what
  the records SAY, which is not automatically what is TRUE — see Dimension A below).
- "**We estimate…**" — a statistical estimate from a sample.
- "**The model assigns…** / **we project…**" — model output.
- "**One plausible explanation is…**" — interpretation.
- "**This would imply…**" — implication.
- "**We think…**" — editorial judgment.
- "**The evidence does not establish…**" — limitation.

## Uncertainty language: three dimensions, and the weakest link caps the verbs

(Other files call this section the "uncertainty ladder" and a claim's capped verb level
its "evidence tier" — the tier is the verb level the weakest dimension allows.)

A single claim's evidence has THREE independent dimensions. Assess each; the claim's
allowed verbs are capped by its weakest dimension, and each dimension adds its own
obligations. (These are dimensions, not exclusive bins — a sampled randomized experiment
is strong on identification AND carries sampling obligations.)

**Dimension A — MEASUREMENT (how the numbers were made).**
- *Administrative/registry count* ("census"): hedge-free verbs apply to the measure AS
  DEFINED — name the frame in or near the claim ("covered employment", "Title-IV
  institutions"). Administrative counts still carry coverage, classification, and
  processing error: what you may assert hedge-free is "the registry recorded X."
  First-print values from revision-prone series (QCEW, trade data) take estimate
  language until revisions settle, and any record/highest-since claim must survive the
  question "could a past-revision-sized change erase it?"
- *Small counts*: a registry count is exact about what happened, not about the process —
  trend or risk verbs resting on small counts ("deaths doubled from 4 to 8") drop to
  estimate language or carry an explicit note that the change is within normal
  year-to-year variation for a count that small.
- *Sample estimate* (surveys, polls, samples): "about", "roughly", "an estimated X";
  report the interval at first use WITH its confidence level ("44%, ±3 points at 95%"),
  and note the margin covers sampling error only. **Comparisons of two estimates**: the
  interval that matters is the interval on the DIFFERENCE — a poll lead's margin is
  roughly double the reported ± for one share, and overlapping intervals do not mean no
  difference. A lead inside the difference's margin is written as within the noise.

**Dimension B — SCOPE (how far the numbers generalize).**
- *Whole small population* (≤~30 units or a few time points): the descriptive fact is
  exact; the PATTERN claim is not — process noise replaces sampling error. Cap trend and
  explanatory verbs at association level unless the pattern holds unit-by-unit (and show
  that); note when a single unit can move the aggregate.
- *Selected extremes*: a unit chosen as the max/min of many scanned cells carries the
  winner's-curse warning (SKILL.md stage 3) — say the ranking is unstable or show
  persistence.

**Dimension C — IDENTIFICATION (what kind of relationship you may assert).**
- *Association only* (observational): "is associated with", "is linked to", "tends to",
  "tracks with". Ban "causes", "leads to", "because of", "drives". ("Predicts" belongs
  to model claims, below.)
- *Named AND probed identification* (experiment, natural experiment, discontinuity —
  the design named in text AND its key assumption stated with at least one recorded
  check: parallel pre-trends, no bunching at the cutoff, instrument plausibility):
  causal verbs allowed — "causes", "raises", "reduces". Naming a design without probing
  it earns nothing.
- *Model/forecast output*: "the model estimates", "we project", "X has a Y% chance".
  Whole-percent probabilities (71%, never 71.4%). Label the unit at every mention and on
  every graphic — "a 71% chance of winning", never a bare "71%" a reader can take for a
  vote share. For the piece's LOAD-BEARING probability, at first use, give the
  complement and a frequency restatement ("71% — meaning roughly 3-in-10 it goes the
  other way"); a cultural analogy (batting averages) only where it fits the audience.
  Subsequent probabilities carry the complement only where misreading is consequential —
  five baseball analogies per piece is hedge-repetition with a bat. Never round a
  probability into a verdict ("will win", "is safe"). Draw forecast intervals as
  computed — they usually widen with horizon; don't force it.
- *Speculation/hypothesis*: "may", "could", "one explanation is" — attributed to someone
  or flagged as the author's conjecture.

**Global bans at every level**: "proves"; "skyrocketed"/"plummeted" (give the number);
"statistically significant" as a synonym for "large"; a point estimate without its
uncertainty at first mention (where a dimension above requires one); a relative risk or
percent change in a rare outcome without its absolute base in the same breath ("doubles
a 1-in-10,000 risk — to 2-in-10,000"); percent/percentage-point confusion.

**Claim-type quick map** (claim type from pitch Q9 → which dimensions bind): descriptive
→ A (+B if small/selected); comparative → A, and the difference-interval rule; explanatory
→ C at association level unless identification is probed; predictive → C's model rules;
causal → C's identification bar, else demote the claim to association language; normative
("should") → not an evidence claim — attribute as editorial judgment ("we think") or to a
named advocate.

Calibrate small differences against noise: a trend claim resting on 1.20 → 1.21 needs
the observed range or revision history shown, or the claim softened. If a series is
known-volatile, the revision caveat sits next to the claim it affects, sized to whether
past revisions could erase the claimed change.

## Human scale (a beat the argument needs, not a quota)

At least one moment grounds the aggregate in a person, firm, plant, product, or place —
UNLESS the archetype already puts the reader in the data (lookup and participatory
pieces are at human scale by construction). The ladder, best first:

1. **Reported voice** — one paragraph from someone who lives the phenomenon. Required
   only when the commission, the allegations, or the affected community make reported
   sourcing necessary; otherwise it is the aspiration, not the gate. When the outlet has
   reachable members and the piece would clearly gain, say so in the draft.
2. **Named public instance** — a firm, plant, or event already in the public record,
   sourced outside the claims apparatus.
3. **Concrete translated vignette** — arithmetic the reader can feel: "at a typical
   150-person molder, 10.8% quarterly churn is about five departures and five starts
   every month"; label composites as illustrative. Use the translation the argument
   needs — a dutiful per-typical-firm computation bolted onto every piece reads as
   filler.

A rung-3 vignette SATISFIES the human-scale beat for ONE piece. **Agent escape hatch**
(for rung 1 only): an agent that cannot phone anyone does not fake reporting — it drafts
the interview ask (who to call, the three questions, where the quote slots in) and flags
the piece as either ready-to-ship-at-rung-3 or, when reporting is genuinely required
(allegations, named-subject criticism), ship-blocked-pending-reporting.

**In INSTITUTIONAL mode (pitch Q12), the ladder stops here**: a labeled vignette or a
public named instance satisfies the beat permanently, and the portfolio limit below does
not apply. Credibility rests on checkable transparency instead of on sourcing. What does
still apply: do not dress a composite as reporting — label it, and never let "a life,
not a ratio" introduce another cell average without saying so.

**In JOURNALISM mode the hatch has a portfolio limit, because an escape hatch that is
always taken is just the road.** A body of work in which EVERY piece shipped at rung 3
has a reporting deficit, not a style; outside readers name it immediately ("no named worker, employer,
recruiter, economist or official anywhere"), and labeled composites make it worse, not
better — "illustrative" printed next to a person-shaped example advertises that nobody
was called. Track it: when a third consecutive piece ships at rung 3, the next piece
does not publish until one finding has been reported through a named person or
institution that can explain, contest, or live the mechanism. That single rule is what
outside reviewers consistently name as the highest-leverage change available to a
data-analysis shop that wants to be read as a desk.

## Bylines, credits, and disclosing the machine

The byline names who stands behind the work: a named human or a named desk a reader
could write to, the analysis and graphics credits, the data source, the month.

**Never advertise the absence of accountability.** "Reviewed by a person",
"AI-assisted, human-checked" and similar hedges read as the institution declining to
stand behind its own page — outside editors call this fatal masthead copy, and they are
right: it answers a question the reader had not yet asked, in the weakest available way.

**Disclose the method confidently, in the methodology box, as a claim about rigor rather
than an apology for it.** Say what was scripted, what was re-derived from source, what
was machine-checked, and what a human decided. Where AI assistance is itself part of the
story — a piece whose premise is what public data plus a model can produce — that
disclosure belongs in the standfirst as a stated capability, not buried: it is the
demonstration, and hedging it forfeits the point. The test either way: a reader finishes
the sentence knowing more about how the work was verified, not less about who is
responsible for it.

**One wording, every page, and enforced.** A disclosure stated five ways across a site
reads as five different arrangements, and the drift runs one direction: toward dropping
it. In one audited site half the pages had lost it from the byline, and four credited a
named person for analysis a model produced. The misattributed human is the graver of
those two failures, and the easier one to introduce by accident.

The form that holds up is the ordinary newsroom one: **a named person bylines the page
and answers for it, and the model is credited with what it did.** "By Jane Roe, Metro
desk · Analysis and graphics by <model>". It is shorter than any hedge, it survives
being read aloud, and it puts a name where a reader can aim a complaint. Write it once,
emit it from one place, and assert both halves (Layer 1.5): a page can lose the model
credit or lose the human, and each failure needs its own check. Where a byline is
generated at runtime, the assertion reads the rendered page, because a source grep
cannot see it.

Human examples illuminate mechanism and stakes; they are never anecdotal proof of the
population claim.

## Provenance sentence (in-body, first third)

One sentence, within the first third, separate from the methodology box: "The data were
[compiled] by [SOURCE], [one clause on what the measure includes/excludes]; the analysis
was done by [WHO] and reviewed by [INDEPENDENT CHECKER]." If nobody independent reviewed
it, do not fake it — point to where the data/code can be inspected; if neither review
nor inspection is possible (licensed data), say so and name what a reader CAN verify
(the source's own published totals).

## Methodology box structure

Append under "How we did this", placed after the story's final beat and before the
closer/footer (see page-design.md for the canonical order). Scale to consequence:
culture pieces need items 1–3; anything touching health, money, elections, or policy
needs all seven; a piece spanning domains includes every item any domain triggers.

1. **DATA**: "[Dataset], [compiler], covering [period/geography/N]. [What the measure
   counts and notably excludes.]" One entry per source, with exact filter values. For
   polls/surveys add: sponsor, exact question wording, field dates, N, mode,
   weighting, and the margin with its confidence level.
2. **DEFINITIONS**: every constructed category or threshold, stated operationally.
3. **TRANSFORMATIONS**: adjustments (inflation, per-capita, seasonal handling,
   smoothing, exclusions) and rows dropped, with counts.
4. **CONTESTABLE CHOICES**: for each consequential choice, the alternative rejected and
   why.
5. **UNCERTAINTY**: data vintage and limits, margins where applicable, what would change
   the conclusion.
6. **WHAT THIS DOESN'T SHOW**: 1–3 sentences of explicit scope limits.
7. **REVIEW & REPRODUCIBILITY**: who checked it; link to data/code if releasable; if
   not, one sentence on why.

Reproducibility is a ladder: named sources → detailed methodology → downloadable cleaned
data → analysis scripts → end-to-end pipeline. Climb as high as privacy/licensing allow.

Guardrail placement rule: each guardrail is stated once PER INDEPENDENT READING CONTEXT
— the page prose, a self-contained figure (whose source line carries its own major
exclusion so it survives screenshotting), and the methodology box are three contexts.
Never twice within one context.

## Corrections and updates (different logs)

- **Corrections** (a published number or claim was wrong): dated, append-only, never
  silent — what it was, what it is, what caused the change. Keep READMEs and hub cards
  in sync with corrected pages.
