# Duarte frameworks — cited distillation

Distilled from the author's own copies of the books, read for this skill 2026-08-31.

**When this file is not enough, read the book.** Two Duarte works are not distilled
here (*How to Move Your Audience*, *Presenting Like a Pro*). One caveat learned building
this skill: *DataStory*'s most operational content lives in FIGURES (the DataPOV diagram,
the Recommendation Tree, the verb tables) that full-text search cannot surface — a null
search there is not proof of absence; open the chapter's images. Cite keys: **R p.N** = *Resonate* (2010), printed page. **S p.N**
= *slide:ology* (2008), printed page. **DS Ch.N** = *DataStory* (2019), chapter (EPUB,
no stable pages). **HBR p.N** = *HBR Guide to Persuasive Presentations* (2012), PDF page
(no printed numbers in that conversion). Where the house departs from Duarte, the
departure is labeled — never silently blended.

## 0. How the three books divide the job

*Resonate* = the talk's structure and emotional physics. *DataStory* = turning analysis
into a recommendation (the closest book to this house's work). *slide:ology* = the
slides themselves. A house build usually runs DataStory's arc inside Resonate's
sparkline, rendered under slide:ology's rules.

## 1. Audience

**Hero/mentor stance.** The audience is the hero; you are the mentor — Yoda, not Luke
(R p.20). The mentor's two jobs are teaching and gift-giving (R p.66); the test: the
audience leaves knowing something new AND able to apply it. "The people in your
audience came to see what you can do for them, not what they must do for you" (HBR
p.23). Kill the about-us opener; it goes in a handout (R p.19).

**Profiles.** Six-dimension hero profile: lifestyle, knowledge, motivation/desire,
values, influence, respect (R p.65) — keep answering until you're past what they DO
and into who they ARE (p.64). Seven-question map (HBR p.30): what are they like · why
are they here · what keeps them up at night · how can you solve their problems · what
do you want them to do · how might they resist · how can you best reach them.
Segmentation lenses: politics, demographics, psychographics, firmographics,
ethnographics (HBR p.25) — then write to the single most influential person in the
room without alienating the rest.

**Common ground** (R pp.69–71): shared experiences + common goals + your
qualifications (the similar journey you completed). Establishing how you're alike
also clarifies how you're different.

**Communicating up** (DS Ch.II). Tiers: peers (speak your geek) → managers (crisp
recommendation + comprehensive appendix, they must defend it) → executives (brief,
rigorous, skimmable, THEIR style). Time: **given 30 minutes, formally prepare 15** —
executives interrupt "way before you finish" and that is normal; let them. Trim what
you present, never what you researched — the cut material is held for recall under
questioning. **The six executive levers in three categories** (DS Ch.II figure — the
book's prose says six in one place and three in another; the figure resolves it:
three categories, each with an up and a down): MONEY (revenue/profit ↑, costs ↓),
MARKET (share ↑, time-to-market ↓), EXPOSURE (retention ↑, risk ↓). A recommendation
bound for an executive desk must visibly move one.

## 2. Big Idea → DataPOV

**Big Idea, three components** (R pp.78–79): a unique point of view (not a topic) +
what's at stake + a complete sentence (noun and verb; better with "you" in it).
Emotional test: it must raise pain/lower pleasure for rejecting, or the reverse for
accepting; calibrate gravity to the actual severity, "no more, no less." Worked
escalation (HBR p.39): "Software updates" (topic) → "Your department needs to update
its workflow software" (POV, no stakes) → "Your department will struggle to meet key
production deadlines until we update the workflow software" (both).

**DataPOV** (DS Ch.III) — the Big Idea specialized for data work, "the centerpiece of
your entire recommendation": POV derived from the data + quantified stakes, in a
complete sentence, and it becomes the title of the deliverable. Two elements are
**figure-sourced** (read from her Ch. III diagram "Structure a DataPOV as a Big Idea"
and the Appendix tree, not present in body text — flagged per the cite-verification
pass): the include-the-desired-outcome move, and her contrast pair — "Changing the
shopping cart experience and shipping policies could increase sales by 40 percent"
(DataPOV) vs. "Fix our online shopping cart" (a task, not a POV).

## 3. Structure — the sparkline and the three acts

**The presentation form** (R pp.36–44): begin by establishing *what is* — "a concise
formulation of what everyone agrees is true," **max 10% of total time** (p.38). Turning
point 1 (call to adventure): state *what could be* against it, explicitly, so the gap
appears — the inciting imbalance (p.39). Middle: alternate the two states continuously
(p.40). Turning point 2 (call to action) signals the close (p.42). End in **new
bliss** — a plane HIGHER than the opening: repeat the key points, describe the world
after adoption, assert the idea is right, not merely possible (p.44, principle of
recency). Never end on a to-do list (p.42). The turn is a state change made by
juxtaposition, not an announced transition ("now let's move to what could be" is the
contrived-gap failure, HBR pp.67–74).

**The three-act DataStory** (DS Ch.IV) — the executive-summary form: **Situation**
("there is a problem/opportunity in the data") — *but/and* — **Complication** ("the
data presents problems/opportunities that make proceeding messy") — *so* —
**Resolution** (= the DataPOV). Her worked example (figure-sourced, Ch. IV template
diagram): "Average renewal per region is 62%" — but — "only 23% of western clients
renew" — so — "tailor content to regional preferences to gain share in the west." Act 2 must contain **the measurements that
will change** if the recommendation is approved (six directions: reverse, continue,
increase, reduce, speed up, slow down). Conjunction bank in the DS Appendix.

**Which structure when (house rule):** the three acts are the SUMMARY and the leave-
behind's spine; the sparkline is the LIVE TALK's spine. They nest: acts 1–2 live in
what-is beats, act 3 opens the what-could-be run.

**Measured exemplars** (why the alternation is a law, not a taste): Heritage &
Greatbatch, 476 political speeches, 19,000+ sentences — **half of all applause bursts
land on a contrast** (R p.104). MLK's "I Have a Dream": 27 applause events in 16
minutes — one per ~35 seconds — riding rapid what-is/what-could-be alternation, three
pacing crescendos, hymn-like late repetition (R p.204). Jobs's iPhone launch: bulk of
the talk in what-could-be with repeated returns to what-is for magnitude; 15 minutes
of hardware with the device OFF before the first power-up gasp; demo task varied 47
times, roughly a new feature every 30 seconds (R pp.163–165).

## 4. Contrast toolbox

Three types (R p.40): **content** (what-is vs what-could-be; your view vs theirs —
twelve-pair springboard on p.105: pain/gain, problem/solution, impossible/possible,
information/insight, need/fulfillment…), **emotional** (analytical vs emotional
material — two-column inventory p.136; "data is purely analytical until you explain
why the ups and downs exist"), **delivery** (traditional vs nontraditional across
stage, style, visuals, interaction, content, involvement — p.139; "the key to getting
and holding attention is having something new happen continually," p.138). When the
material is stuck inside what-is (technical teaching), zoom in: Feynman alternates
fact and context, itemized signal and wonderment, inside the flat line (R pp.130–133).

## 5. Sacrifice, resistance, reward

Resistance IS the sacrifice they can see coming (R p.84) — at minimum you ask them to
abandon what they held as true, and even time is "an irretrievable slice of their
lives." Reframe resistance as valor: they are protecting reputation and credibility
(p.86). **Inoculate**: enumerate every way they might resist and state the opposing
points before they can raise them (p.86). Sort resistance into logical / emotional /
practical (HBR p.44); practical resistance gets the shouldered burden — show you're
carrying part of it (freeze your own salary too). **Reward must be proportional to
the sacrifice** (R p.88); seven reward categories (basic needs, security, savings,
prize, recognition, relationship, destiny) and three beneficiaries (them, their
sphere, mankind — p.89).

## 6. Data → meaning

"Numbers rarely speak for themselves" (R p.117). Three narrative devices: **Scale**
(contrast with familiar sizes), **Compare** (a same-magnitude number from another
domain), **Context** (name what drove the ups and downs).

**Scale doctrine** (DS Ch.IX): "Data should always be precise. When trying to help
others understand magnitude, though, exactitude isn't the point" — the comparison is
deliberately approximate (her stadium example prints the absurd
23.85780746749374 to make that point). People sense scale through **size, distance,
time, speed** (+ quantity); anchors must sit inside lived experience (a car lane, a
sitcom episode, a stadium, boiling an egg). What fails: **weight** (brain infers it
from size), **height** beyond eye-level range, **micro-measurements** (fix: how many
fit in a relatable container). Even a good comparison fails on an unrelatable anchor:
Tyson's dollars-to-the-Moon-and-back flunks because the Moon's distance is itself
unfelt; her rewrite lands on "driving back and forth across the United States 3.4
times." The comparison can encode the IMPROVEMENT, not just the number: data
processing that fell from fly-to-Sydney to fly-to-LA.

**The empirical warrant**: statistics stated bare vs. stated with personal meaning —
by day's end nearly all meaning-attached numbers are recalled, almost none of the
bare ones (DS Ch.X workshop). Heath's Stanford version: 5% remembered a statistic,
63% remembered the stories (DS Introduction).

## 7. Charts, observations, annotation

**Explain with bar, pie, or line only** (DS Ch.VI) — "for gaining buy-in, clarity
always outperforms cool." Two named risks of complex charts: the insight gets buried,
and complex visualizations "look so authoritative that they lead people to suspend
their judgment" — do not look more certain than you are. (Exception: a complex form
that is genuinely your industry's vernacular.)

**Title vs. observation — Duarte's split** (DS Ch.VI): the CHART TITLE is neutral and
factual — the measured noun, precisely, and the when; units on the axis ("2019
Monthly Profit by Percentage," never "We hit our profit goal!"). The CLAIM lives in a
separate **observation** placed above the title, as the slide title, or as a subhead.
Framing an observation is a choice among true statements, and it tells the reader
what to focus on. **House mapping:** this is the evidence-room split — claim in
`.fig-title` position, neutral measurement line in the sub — carried into slides: the
observation takes the slide-title slot; the chart keeps its neutral title beneath.

**Grammar-matched observations** (DS Ch.VI): bar/component charts take ADJECTIVES
(largest/smallest, majority/minority); line charts take VERBS + ADVERBS ("a trend
line is a verb": climb, spike, plummet, converge), with the adverb intensity ladder
calibrated to actual slope — dramatically/sharply → substantially/considerably →
moderately/gradually → slightly/fractionally. Describing the physical shape works
too: a ski jump, breathing in then exhaling. **House note:** this is the licensed
register for talking about a chart; naming rules and rails ("the line down the left
edge") stays banned chart-speak in both lanes.

**Annotation taxonomy** (DS Ch.VII), five moves: highlight (gray the rest, one pop of
color) · label (make the number huge; a donut is a pie with its key segment annotated
in the center) · bracket (connect two points, show the math) · delineate (benchmark
or goal line plus the shortfall math) · explode (break a subcategory into a secondary
chart). Do the interpretive work FOR the reader — a chart that makes the audience
hover to find the insight gets re-charted with the insight annotated (her billable-
hours case).

**Data-slide rules** (S pp.64–75): "Data slides are not really about the data. They
are about the meaning of the data" (p.64). Five rules: tell the truth (no decoration
— "ornamentation can detract from credibility"; no 3-D, no tilted pies, axes honest)
· get to the point (articulate the conclusion first; the conclusion determines the
chart and its emphasis) · pick the right tool (pie/bar/line workhorses; "sometimes
the best chart is no chart at all" — one number, huge; pies start at 12 o'clock, ≤8
sections; align axes across consecutive slides) · highlight what's important — the
**three-layer model**: background (ticks, grids, legends — neutral), data (legible at
distance), emphasis (the single key message) · keep it simple (Tufte's "chartjunk"
cited by name, p.74; identifiers in the graph instead of a legend; never table +
chart of the same numbers on one slide). When the audience genuinely needs to
examine data and draw their own conclusions, print it — slides are the wrong medium
(p.64). Complex-data statistics that must MOVE people: illustrate them as icons,
isolate them as large lone statements, use real photos over stock (S pp.78–79).

## 8. STAR moments and sound bites

**S.T.A.R. = Something They'll Always Remember** (R p.148): planted deliberately so
the talk survives its own ending — the watercooler retelling, the headline. Five
types: memorable dramatization · repeatable sound bites · evocative visuals · emotive
storytelling · shocking statistics. Constraints: significant, sincere, enlightening;
magnifies the big idea rather than distracting; matched to the audience ("don't
create something overly emotionally charged for an audience of biochemists").
Quantity: one intentional per presentation (p.148); Jobs ran four across a keynote of
"an hour and a half or more" (p.163) — **the hard cap of ONE is house doctrine for
15–40 minute rooms, not Duarte's rule.** Placement: no stated rule; her measured cases sit in the back half
at an emotional peak (Zander two-thirds through; HBR p.81 says "climactic").
Exemplars: Feynman's O-ring in ice water; Gates's mosquitoes; the MacBook Air
envelope; Pollan's 26 ounces of crude beside one cheeseburger.

**Sound-bite mechanics** (R p.152): repeat the phrase more than once; pause long
enough to write it down; project the words. Coordinate the phrase verbatim with the
press release; build one obviously broadcast-ready 15–30 second segment. Jobs said
"reinvent the phone" five times, matching Apple's release; it ran as next-day
headlines (p.163). Rhetorical devices: anaphora, mesodiplosis, epistrophe (pp.152–3).

## 9. Verbs — the recommendation's engine (DataStory's distinctive contribution)

Verb modalities: **Change / Continue / Finish** (DS Ch.III). **Performance verbs**
(move a KPI, continuous: capture, disrupt, grow, reduce, shift…) vs. **process verbs**
(complete-or-not: build, conduct, implement, evaluate…). **Escalation rule: if an
executive approves it, lead with a performance verb** — process verbs nest beneath as
sub-actions (DISRUPT → research/develop/invest). Either can be strategic at scale
("build a plant in Illinois to save $6M/yr"). Grammar system for data prose: nouns =
what you measure (say precisely which noun, how, when); adjectives describe static
(bar/component) data; adverbs describe trend lines; conjunctions (but/and/so) drive
the acts; interjections are for the live voice.

## 10. Slides and the leave-behind

**Glance test** (S p.140): slides are billboard media — the message processes in
**3 seconds** or the slide fails. (No separate 5-second data-slide test exists in the
book — verified absent.) Never more than ~2 minutes on one slide; a slide should
process in under 3 seconds; no right slide count — "as many as necessary" (R
pp.178–179; S p.236: "use as many slides as you need… one point per slide").

**Slideument thresholds** (S pp.6–7): >75 words on a slide = a document; ~50 = a
teleprompter. The ban's basis is cognitive: audiences listen OR read, never both.
The PowerPoint default template IS a slideument (p.144).

**The leave-behind is a Slidedoc** (DS Ch.VIII) — a visual document DESIGNED to be
read, not presented: cover = the DataPOV as title · executive summary = the three
acts in full sentences, no bullets · body = the Recommendation Tree, one idea per
slide, skimmable · appendix = everything else. **Never present a Slidedoc while
talking**; if forced to project it, go silent while they read. This resolves the
slideument problem by splitting the artifact, not by thinning the deck.

**Recommendation Tree** (DS Ch.V/VIII/Appendix): root = the three-act DataStory;
row 2 = sub-actions finishing "Therefore, we need to… What? What? What?" (three by
convention, not rule); rows 3+ = WHAT (the action, verbs) / WHY ("Because…" — the
data that must change) / HOW (process verbs to completion). One rectangle = one
slide. The one-page version compresses this to a summary band + a 3×3 WHAT/WHY/HOW
grid — the template behind this house's one-page ask.

**Arrangement, six principles** (S p.92), each an audience outcome: contrast (find
the point fast) · flow (know the processing order — the Z-path) · hierarchy (see
relationships) · unity (sense it belongs together — the grid) · proximity (read
meaning from position) · whitespace (breathing room — "clutter is a failure of
design," p.106). Removal test: "What can I take away that won't change the meaning?"
Tufte's smallest effective difference governs contrast (p.95): every stylistic
difference signals importance, so every one must be intentional.

**Diagram taxonomy** (S pp.44–45), when a concept needs a form: abstract — flow,
structure, cluster, radiate; realistic — pictorial, display-data. Pick by the
relationship, not by what looks impressive.

## 11. The skeptic's apparatus (what a recommendation must carry)

From DS Ch.V — these are REQUIRED CONTENTS of the deliverable, not private prep:
- **Notable objections, in the artifact**: "If you don't thoughtfully present
  alternative perspectives, your audience is likely to think you failed to consider
  them." State the counterargument, then rebut it explicitly.
- **The abandoned alternatives and why** — executives interrupt partly because they
  suspect a different direction; show you explored it and why you rejected it.
- **Assumptions as "This is true if…"** — every prediction names its run-rate,
  economic, hiring, and competitor assumptions in that form, each defensible.
- Probe your own cause story with a repeated "…but why?" before trusting it (her
  root-cause move; the formal "five whys" count is Toyota's, not hers); don't
  over-pile the WHY evidence.

## 12. Editing and rehearsal doctrine

Diverge then converge; the clever ideas appear in round three or four of generation
(R p.98). **The Big Idea is the filter** — cut everything that doesn't distinctly
support it (p.119, Rule #5 p.123); "make edits on behalf of the audience… be severe
in your cuts"; but re-check that contrast survived the cutting floor (p.119). Topics
→ messages: cluster MECE into 3–5 topics, then convert each into a full, emotionally
charged sentence — "topics are neutral; messages are charged" (pp.120–121). Slide-
title outline test (HBR p.53): read only the titles — do they carry the argument?
Titles active, with verbs.

Timing: plan content for **about two-thirds of the slot** (R p.176: "if they give you
an hour, target a talk at forty minutes"; HBR p.93 labels that same 40-of-60 as "60%"
— her arithmetic slip, carried honestly: the operative rule is leave a third of the
slot for the room); build a false ending (summary) and a real one (the close)
so overruns cost the summary, never the close; rehearse against a clock counting up
while cutting, a timer counting down once inside, quarters marked. Audience tolerance
runs 30–40 minutes; "people won't scold you for ending early" (HBR p.93). Screening:
a panel profiled like the target audience, deliberately including naysayers, budgeted
at **3× the talk's length** (R p.182). Letting go of text: reduce to keywords then to
images; the cut words were YOUR safety net, not their content (S pp.220–222).

## 13. Why story structure for numbers at all (the science, briefly)

As Duarte reports the literature (DS Introduction; the citations are hers, not
independently verified by this house): stories engage sensory and emotional
processing beyond language areas (Rutledge 2011); synchronize speaker and listener
brain activity (Nummenmaa 2014); shift attention from critical processing toward
positive affect (Escalas 2004); and alter brain chemistry — cortisol and oxytocin
(Zak). Treat these as her warrant for story structure, not as settled mechanism. The
house's own reason for the BOUNDARY LAW is simpler and does not lean on
neuroscience: a story is selection and ordering in service of an action, so the
numbers it carries must have survived hostile review somewhere the story has no
influence — and the room must always be able to check.
