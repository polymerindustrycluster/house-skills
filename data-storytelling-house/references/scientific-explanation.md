# Scientific explanation in a room

Use when the audience needs to understand a mechanism, process, model, or unfamiliar
scientific concept. Keep the room audit, evidence boundary, distribution contract,
and chosen room shape. A short explanation inside a pitch does not change the
whole talk into a workshop. This reference does not authorize articles or evidence
pages for any readership.

## Define what understanding would look like

Write a specific outcome: what should a listener be able to explain, distinguish,
predict, or apply? "Understand polymers" is too broad. "Explain why this observation
does not yet establish performance in the intended application" is testable.
Check the audience's starting knowledge; a job title is not a knowledge test.

Choose the prerequisites that the explanation actually depends on. Before combining
unfamiliar components, establish their names, locations, and possible states or
behaviors, along with the needed units and notation. A glossary alone may not supply
that component model. Experts may need only the unfamiliar part. For a mixed room, use the audience-translation
reference to establish a shared entry point and optional depth.

## Build a guided explanation

The following is a **house planning scaffold**, not a required sequence or an
author's named framework. Use only the parts the audience needs:

1. **Prerequisites:** establish the objects, terms, scale, and question.
2. **Mechanism:** show the supported relationships and what changes what. If the
   mechanism is unknown or contested, show that uncertainty; do not invent an
   explanation to fill the diagram.
3. **Evidence:** connect the explanation to what was observed or measured. A
   plausible mechanism alone does not establish that it caused an observed result.
4. **Limits:** retain conditions and alternative explanations that change the
   audience's interpretation. Put material limits with the claim they qualify.
5. **Application:** apply the concept to the room's task, or state what remains
   unknown before anyone can use it. Do not invent a commercial benefit or ask.

For a review, usually open with the question and bounded result, then explain what
is needed to judge them. For teaching, a complex claim, or a skeptical room, a
question can lead into evidence before the assertion is revealed (Alley, pp. 188–191).
Give the audience time to inspect the evidence, then state the supported conclusion
and its limits. For a lesson, allow the time needed to establish prerequisites.
The pitch's opening-time target is not a cap on learning a mechanism.

## Make the visual earn its place

- Use a complete, evidence-calibrated message with visual support; a question-led
  teaching sequence may reveal the assertion after the evidence. Retain labels,
  units, and qualifications needed to read that support correctly.
- Choose the representation for the relationship: process stages, component
  interactions, a comparison, or a measurement. A schematic is not measured data;
  label it as a schematic, conceptual model, hypothesis, or not-to-scale drawing
  where that distinction matters.
- Give an initial overview of an intricate diagram or equation, then introduce its
  major parts while keeping names, positions, and encodings consistent. Locate a
  close-up within its parent view; return to the whole so the relationship is recoverable.
  Static successive slides can do this; animation is optional and must explain a
  change, not merely decorate it. Preserve a usable static version for export.
- Use the overview to orient, not to make novices decode the whole mechanism first.
  Signal the part and relationship being explained with labels, highlights, or
  spoken connectors. Attention alone is not understanding; causal language still
  requires causal support from the scientific source.
- Place labels beside their referents and coordinate the spoken explanation with
  the relevant visual. Avoid narrating unrelated prose while the room decodes it.
  Accessibility captions, essential labels, and readable leave-behinds remain valid;
  do not turn advice about redundant prose into a ban on accessible text.
- Use the brief glance test for the takeaway and focal point. Allow guided viewing
  time for the mechanism itself, then test understanding separately. A word or
  numeral cap must not remove a condition that changes the scientific conclusion:
  split or sequence the explanation and use a suitable readable artifact.

Use existing chart, slide, and schematic production tools as appropriate. The
scientific content still needs source checking; an attractive generated diagram
is not evidence that its mechanism is correct.

For a process, choose the critical states, keep the tracked object identifiable,
and retain the condition behind a conclusion-changing transition. For a comparison,
name the relationship to judge and align corresponding states, parts, or conditions.
Keep group identities consistent; distinguish a qualitative illustration from a
measured difference. When physical size is the comparison, give a common scale or
explicit size references so equal display size cannot imply equal physical size.

Decode visual jargon as well as words. Check what each arrow, line, symbol, or
color means to this audience: sequence, movement, activation, inhibition, and
causation are different relationships. Explain unfamiliar conventions beside their
referents; a leader line need not imply a process. Where a transition or connection
is unknown, identify that gap or tentative relationship explicitly rather than
finishing the mechanism by invention. Explain any uncertainty encoding; a question
mark cannot replace supplied quantitative uncertainty. These are selective
applications of Christiansen (pp. 66–67, 174–190), within the house evidence boundary.

When a suitable familiar representation helps, map the abstraction to it; otherwise
introduce its symbols and relationships directly. State what any simplification
retains and omits. These are selective applications of Frankel and DePace's visual
strategies, not a mandatory diagram template. Keep a recoverable overview and short
slide message; speech should carry the main relationship. A live visual may rely
on narration for detail. A circulated version must carry the context and material
qualifiers needed to interpret it correctly (Christiansen, pp. 278–282). These
checks supplement the comprehension test; they do not certify accessibility.

For a difficult explanation, pause at meaningful state changes or reasoning steps
and use comprehension evidence to adjust pace. Presenter pauses are an adaptation
of multimedia segmenting research, not individual learner control. The original
Mayer–Chandler experiment could not separate control from extra study time; it
does not establish a universal seconds-per-slide rule for this room. Retain concise
action labels and inspectable technical terms when useful. Short labels, a full
narration transcript, and captions have different jobs: Mayer–Johnson's label study
found a retention advantage without a demonstrated transfer advantage. Choose their
use around the task, pacing, language, and hearing needs, not a blanket text ban.

## Use an analogy with an explicit boundary

When an analogy helps, write down the correspondence (which familiar feature stands
for which scientific feature) and where it fails. Explain that failure out loud
when it could produce a wrong inference. Then return to the actual phenomenon.
If the analogy requires more repairs than the direct explanation, drop it.

Example of the **form**, not a scientific claim: "The analogy helps explain X. It
does not represent Y, so it cannot tell us Z." Never infer material properties,
causality, safety, or application readiness from the analogy.

## Rehearse for comprehension

Write a question and answer criteria before the review. Prefer a small new case or
a changed condition: can the listener use the relationship and identify its limit?
Quoting the headline or naming the components alone does not satisfy a transfer task.

Use [review-gates Layer 3](review-gates.md#layer-3--cold-listener-the-only-layer-that-cant-be-faked)
for unprompted takeaways followed by the comprehension question, withheld answer
criteria, and honest human/AI verdicts. Score the supported relationship, important
boundary, and application; a materially wrong inference needs revision even when
the headline was recalled. Record an unrun human check as unrun.

State what was tested: recall, explanation of a relationship, or application to a
changed case. Participation, fluent Q&A, and perceived ease do not establish
transfer; an immediate rehearsal check does not establish durable learning. If
later use matters, identify a suitable later task and timing without inventing an
evaluation commitment. A small rehearsal is formative feedback, not a controlled
test of instructional effectiveness.

Use the scientific extension to the teach-back card in room-artifacts.md. A source
identifier can substitute for a NUMBER GUARD when a concept has no numeric claim;
do not add arbitrary numbers to a lesson to satisfy the pitch's retention rules.

Prepare likely evidence questions and an honest unknown-answer response. Listen to
the whole question, clarify it for the room when needed, pause, and answer briefly.
Separate what the source establishes from what remains unresolved. If asked about
an interval, state what it represents; spread and uncertainty about an estimate
are not interchangeable. Do not calculate a new estimate or invent a mechanism at
the podium. An unknown answer can name the evidence needed without promising
unapproved follow-up work. This applies Doumont's Q&A practice with the house's
stricter scientific-evidence boundary; his allowance for a labeled guess does not
authorize invented scientific results or application promises.

Rehearse the difficult explanation and the bridge to the next slide or speaker,
not just the opening. Know what follows before advancing; use a brief shared-level
recap or orientation when the specialist topic changes (Alley, pp. 23–25, 54–56,
83–86, 223–224, 231–234). If a question exposes a substantive error, acknowledge it
and identify affected later conclusions (p. 268). This does not authorize guessing
an answer or promising follow-up that no owner has accepted.
