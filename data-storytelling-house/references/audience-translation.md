# Translate evidence for different audiences

Use when adapting one finding for several audiences or preparing a room with mixed
technical knowledge. Keep one source-backed claim while changing entry point,
detail, vocabulary, medium, and the action the actual audience can take.

## Establish what each audience needs to do with it

Use the commissioning brief and current operating context to identify the audience.
For each version, record its knowledge, practical concern, question to answer,
authority to act, intended outcome, and distribution. Name what is confirmed and
what is still an assumption; do not infer knowledge, motives, or decision rights
from someone's title. When a missing fact changes the recommendation or release
boundary, ask; otherwise state a reasonable provisional assumption and proceed.

Also verify the recipient's preferred medium and reading/discussion depth. An
executive may want a detailed report, a short pre-read, or a conversation. A brief
live contribution and a thorough technical appendix can serve the same decision;
short presentation time does not justify shallow preparation (Duarte, *DataStory*,
Ch.II).

Check subject expertise separately from knowledge of this project's situation
(Doumont, printed pp. 36–39). An expert may need the purpose, comparator, and history;
an insider may still need technical terms introduced. A forwarded room artifact
must carry the context its later reader needs without reconstructing the meeting.

For PIC, the following are **starting questions**, not a standing stakeholder roster
or a claim that all members of a role think alike:

| Audience lens | Questions to verify with the brief |
|-|-|
| Technical collaborators | What mechanism, measurement, comparator or uncertainty must they judge? |
| Manufacturers and prospective users | Which use condition, qualification requirement or scale-up constraint matters? |
| Funders and civic audiences | What was achieved, how was it measured, and what benefit is still a forecast? |
| PIC/GAC leadership and colleagues | Which decision, dependency, resource constraint or coordination need is theirs? |
| Members, learners or community participants | What does this mean in their experience, what terms need explaining, and what questions or choices do they have? |

Do not import unrelated private projects or confidential partner details to make a
version more persuasive. Confirm recipient and distribution permissions. Translation
does not authorize sending, posting, or making a commitment.

## Make versions from a common claim record

Use the existing evidence artifact or verified claim file as the common source.
Reference its identifier and version from every audience version. Preserve:

- direction and magnitude of the finding, with the approved room-scaled wording;
- population/material, unit, denominator, comparator, time and test conditions;
- uncertainty and the distinction between observed, modeled and hypothesized;
- limits and counter-evidence that change the conclusion;
- the separation between results already achieved and future application or impact.

This preservation list is a **house adaptation**. It extends the existing NUMBER
GUARD to nonnumeric scientific meaning without creating a second evidence store.
Do not copy a table of authoritative values into every version and let the copies
drift. Reference the shared record; if a value changes, review all dependent versions.

What may change: the opening question, example, definition, depth, order, amount of
backup, speaking time, and legitimate next step. Define unfamiliar language using
the actual concept. Shorter words are not a license to replace a precise claim with
a broader one. Where no action is requested, use a learning, review or FYI outcome.

For example, a fictional bench result can be presented to reviewers as a comparison
under stated test conditions, to a manufacturer as a reason to evaluate its own use
conditions, and to a funder as a completed experiment with a remaining validation
question. It cannot become a proven product benefit, customer acceptance, or jobs
claim merely because a different audience would find that more compelling.

## Handle mixed knowledge and multiple formats

Give a mixed room a common entry point and the minimum shared vocabulary. Then add
the depth needed for the actual decision, with optional technical branches or backup.
Do not assume a single version can omit all detail to satisfy novices and still
support an expert's judgment. If those jobs compete, use a short shared explanation
plus a separately scoped technical discussion or readable pre-read.

When moving between specialist sections or speakers, briefly re-establish the
shared question and explain the connection before descending into new detail;
return to common implications at the end. This applies Alley's mixed-audience and
transition guidance (pp. 23–25, 54–56, 83–86); it is not a fixed agenda for every talk.

Each version inherits its own declared room shape and distribution contract. A live
talk and a readable leave-behind may share evidence but need their own composition.
A request for an article or evidence page, including one for known readers, routes to data-journalism-house;
this module may hand over the claim references and audience brief but does not write
that artifact. A recorded/posted talk follows SKILL.md's existing release safeguards.

Before adapting an existing graphic, distinguish three jobs (Christiansen,
pp. 274–282): **reformatting** retains its content while changing layout or size;
**a new setting** may change what speech, visuals, and readable material each carry;
**a new audience** may need different vocabulary and background. Use the appropriate
production workflow for edits to approved figures. Inspect the actual display size
and what is visible together: keys, referents, and interpretation-critical qualifiers
must be available when needed. A projected slide and a forwardable handout need
separate checks even when their underlying evidence is identical.

## Compare meaning, then test each version

Before rehearsal, compare versions against the common record. Use a compact check:

| Version | Shared claim/source | New emphasis or action | Material qualifiers retained? | Unsupported inference to prevent |
|-|-|-|-|-|
| Named audience | Existing identifier/version | What changes for this room | Where each essential limit appears | What a listener must not conclude |

An unused version need not be built just to fill the table. A real change in scope,
certainty or causal meaning is a failure; a different emphasis is not. Apply the
traceability audit to each artifact, including its headline, figure, speech, and
leave-behind. Essential qualifiers must survive its actual distribution.

Test each selected version using [review-gates Layer 3](review-gates.md#layer-3--cold-listener-the-only-layer-that-cant-be-faked).
For science learning, use a transfer question from scientific-explanation.md; for a
decision, test the boundary from scientific-evidence-review.md. Record untested
audiences as untested rather than generalizing from another version.
