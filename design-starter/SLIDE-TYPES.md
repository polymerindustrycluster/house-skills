# Slide types — the sanctioned vocabulary

Seven slide types cover a standard deck. Each one has exactly one job. Do not
invent an eighth type for a one-off need — fit the content into one of these
seven, or treat the addition as a deliberate, documented extension to the
vocabulary rather than a one-off exception. A deck built entirely from these
seven, following `COMPOSITION.md`, reads as one coherent system instead of
seven improvisations.

For each type below: its job, its dominant element (the thing that must carry
≥2x the visual weight of anything else on the slide, per the composition
law), its density budget, whether it sits on the light or dark surface, what
footer chrome it carries, and the one composition trap that shows up most
often when this type is built carelessly.

## 1. Title

**Job.** Open the deck. Establish what this is, whose meeting or moment it's
for, and when.

**Dominant element.** The deck title, set at the largest display size on the
slide (roughly 90-100px in a 1920x1080 canvas) — nothing else on this slide
competes with it.

**Density budget.** Three text elements only: title, a subtitle line (the
meeting or committee context), and a date. Nothing else.

**Surface.** Light.

**Footer chrome.** Logo, top-left. No page number and no deck-title strip —
the opening slide doesn't need orientation chrome, because it *is* the
orientation.

**Composition trap.** A left-aligned stack of three short text lines leaves
most of the canvas empty. This type must satisfy COMMIT, not FILL: a bold
accent shape (a color-blocked corner, a bleeding motif) has to carry real,
deliberate visual weight opposite the text stack, or the slide reads as a
title floating alone in a mostly-dead frame. A thin, low-opacity accent that
doesn't register at a glance does not satisfy COMMIT.

## 2. Section divider

**Job.** Pace the deck. Mark the start of a new section so the audience
re-orients.

**Dominant element.** A single large section numeral, set enormous (400px+),
low-opacity or tinted so it reads as texture rather than a second headline.

**Density budget.** Minimal by design: an eyebrow label ("Section"), the
numeral, and a short section title. This type earns FILL through scale, not
through content volume — resist the urge to add supporting text.

**Surface.** Dark.

**Footer chrome.** Inverted (light) logo, dimmed deck-title strip, page
number.

**Composition trap.** The giant numeral and the section-title block are two
separate visual masses. If they aren't vertically aligned to a shared center
line, they read as two floating islands rather than one balanced asymmetric
composition — an instance of the "symmetric dead bands above and below a
floating middle cluster" auto-fail from the composition law.

## 3. Content

**Job.** Carry a substantive claim with supporting narrative — the
deck's workhorse for prose explanation.

**Dominant element.** The headline, set as a large multi-line display
statement (a sentence with a verb, not a topic label) — it must out-weigh the
body text underneath it.

**Density budget.** Eyebrow, headline, a short accent rule, then up to four
short paragraphs run in two columns. Four paragraphs is the ceiling, not a
target — each should read as one idea, not a wall of sentences.

**Surface.** Light.

**Footer chrome.** Logo, deck-title strip, page number.

**Composition trap.** Two columns of body-size prose is the easiest way in
this whole vocabulary to accidentally build a text dump. It only holds
together when the headline is large enough to visibly dominate the paragraph
block below it — if the headline shrinks to compete with the body text in
weight, the slide collapses into an undifferentiated wall of reading.

## 4. Stat grid

**Job.** Prove a claim with hero numbers — the "by the numbers" moment.

**Dominant element.** The grid of stat numerals collectively, each set larger
than the slide's own headline — the numerals, not the headline, are what the
audience's eye lands on first.

**Density budget.** Exactly at the numeral ceiling: four stats, one numeral
each, one short label each. Do not add a fifth.

**Surface.** Light.

**Footer chrome.** Logo, deck-title strip, page number.

**Composition trap.** Four co-equal cards with a thin color-coded top border
as the only differentiation can read as a spreadsheet row rather than a
designed hero moment. The numerals have to stay dramatically larger than
their labels (roughly 4-5x the label size) or the grid collapses into a plain
data table with oversized type.

## 5. Two-up (compare)

**Job.** Compare two paths, options, or offers side by side.

**Dominant element.** This type deliberately breaks the single-dominant-
element rule in favor of two balanced masses — each column's own subhead is
the dominant element within its half.

**Density budget.** Eyebrow, a shared headline framing the comparison, then
two columns, each with a category label, a subhead, and up to four short list
items.

**Surface.** Light.

**Footer chrome.** Logo, deck-title strip, page number.

**Composition trap.** If one column's list runs shorter than the other's, the
two columns end at different heights and leave an asymmetric gap at the
bottom of the shorter one. Two columns invite exactly the kind of dead band
the composition law auto-fails — balance the list lengths, or give the
shorter column a closing element (a summary line, a rule) that carries it to
the same baseline as its neighbor.

## 6. Quote

**Job.** Humanize and validate the deck's argument with a real voice —
typically a testimonial or a stakeholder's own words.

**Dominant element.** The quote text itself, set large enough (mid-50px
range) to read as the hero content — not the oversized decorative quotation
mark that frames it.

**Density budget.** One quote (2-3 sentences, no more), one attribution line
(name, role, and affiliation), and one small avatar or initials mark.

**Surface.** A warm neutral, distinct from both the pure-white content
surface and the dark divider surface — this type gets its own quieter
register.

**Footer chrome.** Logo, deck-title strip, page number.

**Composition trap.** The oversized quotation-mark glyph is decoration, not
the dominant element — it's easy to let it dominate by sheer size while the
actual quote sentence stays at ordinary body size. If the glyph is the
biggest thing on the slide, the hierarchy is backwards; the words matter more
than the punctuation that frames them.

## 7. Thank you (closer)

**Job.** Close the deck and leave the audience with where to go next —
contact, web, social.

**Dominant element.** The closing headline itself, set at the largest type
size used anywhere in the deck — this is the one slide where type size alone
should exceed every stat numeral and every section-divider numeral.

**Density budget.** A headline, a one-line attribution ("From [the
organization]."), and a horizontal row of contact/links (web, social, email —
three is typical, more starts to clutter the band).

**Surface.** Dark — bookends the deck against the section-divider surface.

**Footer chrome.** Inverted logo, top-left, only. No page number or
deck-title strip, matching the title slide — the links row itself functions
as the closing footer.

**Composition trap.** A huge headline sitting alone in the upper half of a
dark canvas, with a thin strip of tiny contact text pinned to the very bottom
and nothing filling the space between, is the section-divider dead-band
failure again. The dark surface's own edge-to-edge color has to be doing
compositional work — this type leans on COMMIT (headline mass on one side,
open dark field carrying the brand motif) rather than trying to FILL the
whole canvas with content.
